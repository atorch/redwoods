🌲 About this map

An interactive map of where coastal redwoods could grow along the Northern California coast — based on fog and rainfall.

What question is this answering?

The Wikipedia range map is a static PNG drawn from survey data. It answers: where are redwoods growing today? California State Parks publishes a similar coastal redwood parks map that goes a step further by distinguishing original from current growth — together they sketch the historical and modern footprints.

This map asks a different question: where could redwoods grow today, given enough fog and rainfall? That's very close to asking where redwoods might have existed in the 1700s, before urban development and logging reshaped the landscape — roughly the union of "original" and "current" growth on the State Parks map, but derived from physical conditions (rainfall + fog) rather than survey data, and zoomable instead of static.

Why fog? During the rainless summer, coastal redwoods harvest moisture from fog directly through their needles and drip the rest to the forest floor — supplying up to 40% of their annual water and effectively letting the trees water themselves when no rain is falling. A three-year study in northern California found the canopy itself roughly doubles fog-water input to the ground: about 34% of annual water under redwoods came from fog drip off the trees, versus 17% in nearby areas without them. (See also Save the Redwoods League on the science of redwoods and fog.)

How it works

We follow a simple heuristic: a place likely supported redwoods if it receives at least 20 inches of rain in the wet season, stays inside the maritime temperature envelope, has marine-layer fog overhead on at least 50 nights of the dry season, and is on land rather than open water or wetland.

  • Rainfall — PRISM 800 m wet-season precipitation normals (Nov–Apr).
  • Fog — GOES-16 satellite Channels 7 (3.9 µm) and 13 (10.3 µm). At night, low water clouds emit much less in the shorter-wavelength channel than the longer one, producing a positive brightness-temperature difference (BTD) that uniquely identifies marine-layer fog — the standard NESDIS nighttime fog detection method. We sample four weeks per dry season (May / Jun / Aug / Sep) across 2020–2024 in the 06–12 UTC window — 11 PM – 5 AM PST — when BTD is uncontaminated by solar reflection. A "fog night" is any night with at least one in-window scan flagged as low cloud at that pixel; the count is extrapolated to the 184-day dry season and the 50-night threshold is tuned against ground truth.
  • Temperature — places where the coldest month averages below ~-3 °C or the hottest month averages above ~30 °C are excluded. Coast redwoods are a maritime species; per the USDA Silvics of North America, the native range sits between mean annuals of 10–16 °C with rare drops below -9 °C and rare highs above 38 °C. This filter rejects the cold Cascade / Klamath interior and the hot Central Valley. PRISM 30-year monthly tmin / tmax normals.
  • Land mask — open water and wetlands are dropped using the USDA Cropland Data Layer, so the suitability layer reflects land where redwoods could actually root rather than the pixels of bay and ocean a coastal raster necessarily includes.

Green pixels on the map are places that meet all four criteria. Red dots are ground-truth redwood locations used to sanity-check the model.

Satellites see cloud tops, not whether cloud is actually touching the canopy and dripping moisture onto the forest floor. Nighttime marine-layer presence is a strong proxy for the morning and daytime conditions that produce fog interception in coastal canyons — coastal sites with frequent overnight fog overhead also tend to have lingering canopy contact at dawn — but it is still a proxy. We also evaluated a daytime visible-channel signal and published MODIS / Torregrosa fog rasters; the nighttime BTD layer cleanly separates the redwood ground-truth points from inland control sites, while the other products do not.
